# Translation Craft for Film Dialogue

Professional subtitle translation is constrained dialogue writing. The translator must first understand the whole dramatic exchange, then create the shortest target-language line that preserves the intended effect.

## 1. Decode before writing

For each scene, answer silently:

- Who speaks?
- To whom?
- What do they want from the addressee?
- What are they pretending to want?
- What is at stake?
- What does the addressee know that the audience may not?
- What changes by the end of the scene?
- Which words are literal, ironic, evasive, ritual, or loaded?

Unresolved pronouns, omitted subjects, and mixed speaker boundaries should be solved at scene level before producing polished lines.

## 2. Speech acts

Translate the action performed by the line:

- Command, request, plea, warning, threat.
- Offer, promise, refusal, evasion.
- Insult, joke, flirtation, seduction.
- Confession, accusation, denial, self-justification.
- Ritual formula, oath, prayer, curse.
- Teaching, exposition, negotiation, bargaining.

A line that preserves the words but changes "warning" into "advice" is a mistranslation.

## 3. Pragmatics and subtext

Preserve:

- Implicature: what is meant but not said.
- Politeness and face-saving.
- Social hierarchy in address forms.
- Deliberate vagueness and evasion.
- Repeated words that create pressure or irony.
- Silence, unfinished sentences, and interruptions.

Do not make subtext explicit. If a line works because a character avoids saying something, the subtitle should also avoid saying it.

## 4. Cohesion across cues

Cue boundaries are technical, not dramatic boundaries.

- A pronoun may refer to a noun in a previous scene.
- A one-word answer may depend on the question from the previous cue.
- A conjunction may be carried over several subtitles.
- A punchline may be split across speaker changes.

Do not translate an isolated cue so that it becomes grammatically complete if the completion changes meaning.

## 5. Character voice

Build a voice profile:

```text
Character:
Social position:
Education and profession:
Region/dialect:
Register:
Typical sentence length:
Politeness:
Profanity:
Address forms:
Verbal tics:
Borrowed words:
What they avoid saying:
How their voice changes under stress:
```

Voice distinction should come from:

- Word choice and rhythm.
- Directness versus hesitation.
- Formal versus colloquial grammar.
- Metaphor and idiom.
- Forms of address.
- Profanity and taboo level.
- Amount of explanation.

Do not differentiate every character with a fake dialect or tic. Preserve the screenplay's actual distinctions.

## 6. Names and forms of address

Decide globally:

- Full name, given name, surname, nickname, title, rank, kinship term.
- Whether the source uses a name as intimacy, insult, distance, or ritual.
- Whether a title changes after a relationship or status change.
- Whether a character deliberately uses the wrong name.

Maintain a table:

```text
Source form | Character | Addressee | Relationship | Target form | Notes
```

Do not flatten every `Mr`, `Lord`, `Captain`, `my lady`, and first-name address into one polite Chinese form. The relationship is the meaning.

## 7. Idioms and figurative language

Possible strategies, in order:

1. Use an established target idiom with the same function.
2. Translate the image if it is transparent and natural.
3. Replace with an image native to the target language and scene.
4. Preserve literal wording if its foreignness is meaningful.
5. Explain only when annotation is permitted and necessary.

Avoid forced four-character idioms, proverbs, or internet slang when they clash with period, class, or tone.

## 8. Humor and wordplay

Ask what makes the line funny:

- Sudden reversal.
- Ambiguity.
- Taboo.
- Social embarrassment.
- Misdirected insult.
- Wordplay.
- Repetition and callback.
- Timing.

Preserve the mechanism before the exact word. If a pun cannot survive:

- Build a different target-language pun that serves the same scene.
- Use a functional joke with the same target.
- Keep the literal image and accept lower wordplay.
- Mark the loss internally if it affects later callbacks.

Do not add a joke where the original is serious merely because a pun is untranslatable.

## 9. Profanity and taboo language

Map intensity and social effect, not dictionary equivalence:

```text
mild annoyance < vulgar emphasis < aggressive insult < sexual/religious taboo < extreme degradation
```

Consider:

- Character class and education.
- Target-language region.
- Rating and outlet.
- Whether the word is used affectionately, reflexively, or as a weapon.
- Whether censorship changes the scene.

Avoid using one fashionable swear word for every source-language profanity. Repeated overuse erases character distinction.

## 10. Exposition

Film exposition often sounds unnatural if translated as a complete informational paragraph.

- Keep it embedded in the character's motive.
- Preserve hesitation, persuasion, and conversational framing.
- Avoid adding clauses that explain what the image already shows.
- Verify names and facts.
- Do not sacrifice causal clarity to brevity.

## 11. Action and crisis dialogue

In action scenes:

- Prioritize immediate referents and commands.
- Keep lines short enough to read during movement.
- Do not translate every bark or interjection if it carries no new information.
- Preserve spatial relations: front, behind, above, port, starboard, left, right.
- Keep repeated commands if repetition creates urgency.
- Do not merge two speakers when the distinction is operationally important.

## 12. Narration and internal voice

Decide:

- Is the voice diegetic or non-diegetic?
- Is it retrospective, present-tense, unreliable, or addressed to someone?
- Does the narration know more than the characters?
- Is it lyrical, documentary, clinical, or conversational?

Keep narration distinct from spoken dialogue unless the film intentionally blurs them.

## 13. Lyrics

For songs:

- Mark lyrics consistently.
- Preserve repeated refrain and key images.
- Do not force rhyme unless the user asks for singable lyrics.
- Check whether the song is background texture or plot-bearing.
- Keep song lines shorter than explanatory prose.
- Do not translate a background song so densely that it competes with dialogue.

## 14. On-screen text and signs

Classify:

- Plot-critical sign.
- Establishing location.
- Newspaper/phone/screen text.
- Decorative background text.
- Text the characters read or ignore.

Translate only what the audience needs. Do not invent readable text from an illegible background prop. If the user wants typesetting, preserve position and duration separately from dialogue.

## 15. Multilingual dialogue

Preserve the function of language switching:

- Insiders versus outsiders.
- Secrecy.
- Occupation or diplomacy.
- A character trying to pass as local.
- Deliberate mistranslation.

Options include:

- Translate into the main target language without marking.
- Use italics for a second language if the platform convention supports it.
- Preserve a short phrase and translate nearby.
- Add a speaker/language label only when necessary.

Consistency matters more than choosing a universal convention.

## 16. Condensation

First remove:

- Redundant vocatives.
- Repeated information visible in the image.
- Grammatically obligatory source-language material not needed in the target.
- Empty politeness formulas that carry no relationship information.
- Duplicated subject pronouns.

Do not remove:

- Negation.
- Agency.
- Tense/time markers that change order.
- Uncertainty.
- A lie versus a mistake.
- A character's motive.
- A setup or callback.
- Repeated language that is dramatically meaningful.

Condensation should sound like a shorter version of the same person saying the same thing.

## 17. Revision questions

For every polished line, ask:

1. Does it say the same thing?
2. Does it perform the same speech act?
3. Does it preserve the relationship and power dynamic?
4. Does it sound like this character?
5. Does it fit the available reading time?
6. Does it create an accidental callback or contradiction?
7. Does it explain something the scene deliberately leaves implicit?
8. Would an actor be able to perform it naturally?

If any answer is no, revise or document the unavoidable tradeoff.

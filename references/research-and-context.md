# Research and Context

Research is used to reduce interpretive error, not to make the subtitle sound academic. The goal is to know what a line means to its original audience and to the characters in that fictional world.

## 1. Identify the exact work

Collect:

- Original-language title, translated title, alternate titles, and working titles.
- Year, country, language, director, writer, and production company.
- Film, miniseries, episode, season, and episode number.
- Theatrical/extended/director's-cut/streaming/broadcast version.
- Runtime and distinguishing scene details.
- Whether the text is from a remake, sequel, spin-off, adaptation, or parody.

Use at least two matching identifiers, such as title plus year, or series plus season/episode. Do not research a remake using the original film's glossary.

## 2. Research priority

Use sources in this order, adjusted by the actual evidence:

1. User-provided script, glossary, subtitle, cast list, or platform guide.
2. The exact audio and video supplied by the user.
3. Official screenplay, subtitles, press kit, distributor site, or episode synopsis.
4. Professional subtitle/localization guidance and language authorities.
5. Production interviews, reputable reviews, film databases, and academic/historical references.
6. Fan wikis, fan subtitles, forums, and social posts as discovery leads.

For a consequential fact, prefer two independent reliable sources. A fan wiki may reveal the right question even when it is not sufficient evidence for the answer.

## 3. What to research

### Names and terminology

Verify:

- Character names and pronunciation.
- Real people, gods, historical figures, organizations, and places.
- Military ranks, political titles, religious offices, and institutions.
- Fictional technology, magic systems, factions, species, and honorifics.
- Terms that recur across sequels or episodes.

Choose in this order:

1. Official localized name established by the same release.
2. Standard name in the target language's reputable film/history reference.
3. Recurring official franchise localization.
4. A transparent, pronounceable transliteration with locked spelling.

If two authoritative traditions differ, choose one and record the policy. Do not mix them.

### Historical and social context

Research when relevant:

- Period, location, regime, war, religion, ritual, class system, or law.
- Whether a reference is literal, anachronistic, ironic, or a deliberate fictionalization.
- Forms of address and hierarchy.
- Customs around marriage, hospitality, burial, sacrifice, oath, and honor.
- Weapons, ships, clothing, food, and technology only when they affect dialogue.

Translate the film's diegetic world even when it differs from historical fact. Do not "correct" a character's factual mistake unless the scene requires the correction.

### Cultural references

Classify the reference:

- Necessary to plot.
- Necessary to characterization.
- Decorative or atmospheric.
- Untranslatable wordplay.
- A shared real-world reference the target audience may know.
- A local reference that requires a functional equivalent.

Preserve the function. Add a brief explanation only when:

- The format permits notes.
- The user requests annotation.
- The reference is essential and no concise target-language equivalent exists.

Do not clutter dialogue with explanation that the characters would never say.

### Songs, poems, and quotations

Identify:

- Whether the text is sung, chanted, recited, or read on screen.
- Whether rhyme, meter, alliteration, or refrain matters.
- Whether the song is diegetic or non-diegetic.
- Whether an official translation exists.
- Whether the song foreshadows plot.

If singability is not required, prioritize image, rhythm, and dramatic function. If singability is required, record the tradeoffs and do not force a rhyme that changes meaning.

### Multilingual scenes

Check:

- Which language each character is actually speaking.
- Whether the audience is meant to understand it.
- Whether a character is pretending not to understand.
- Whether the subtitle should translate, transliterate, label, or preserve a foreign phrase.
- Whether an interpreter speaks in the scene.

Do not flatten a language barrier by translating every language into identical unmarked prose.

## 4. Research query strategy

Use several query shapes when identification is uncertain:

- `"exact line" "film title"`
- `"film title" "character name" quote`
- `film title year screenplay`
- `film title episode title cast crew`
- `original-language title official subtitles`
- `character name official name Chinese`
- `term + period/country + translation`

Prefer exact quoted subtitle lines when identifying a version. Search the original-language title, not only the localized title.

When search results are polluted by repeated SEO subtitle sites, seek:

- Publisher/distributor pages.
- Official streaming metadata.
- Published screenplays.
- Reputable film databases.
- Interviews and production notes.
- Scholarly reference works.

Do not copy an online subtitle wholesale merely because it ranks highly. It may be a redistributed machine translation.

## 5. Spoiler control

Research may expose later reveals. Do not leak them into earlier subtitles through over-explanation or loaded terminology.

When a term changes after a reveal:

- Preserve the intended information gradient.
- Avoid using later knowledge to make an earlier line unnaturally explicit.
- Record both the surface meaning and the eventual reveal in the continuity notes.

## 7. Confidence labels

Use:

- **Confirmed**: explicit in authoritative source or audible media.
- **Likely**: strongly supported by context or multiple secondary sources.
- **Unresolved**: conflicting or insufficient evidence.

Do not let a likely inference migrate into a "confirmed" fact later in the report.

## 8. Research failure handling

If the internet or media is unavailable:

- Use the best available source text and context.
- Flag names and cultural references that could not be verified.
- Avoid making irreversible glossary choices when alternatives are equally plausible.
- State which missing evidence limits the score.

Research limitations are not an excuse to leave clear grammar and voice problems unresolved.

# Chinese Film Subtitle Language

Use this reference when translating into Simplified or Traditional Chinese. The principles overlap, but orthography, punctuation, and regional vocabulary must be locked consistently.

## 1. What "natural Chinese" means

Natural subtitle Chinese should sound like speech inside a film, not like a translated article or a literary novel. It should:

- Use information order natural to Chinese.
- Omit pronouns when the referent is clear.
- Prefer verbs with clear agency.
- Use short clauses and recognizable connective logic.
- Avoid compression that makes the line semantically vague.
- Fit the character's period, class, region, and education.

Naturalness is not the same as slanginess. A historical queen, a soldier, and a teenager should not all speak in identical modern internet Chinese.

## 2. Remove translationese

### Excessive pronouns

Weak:

> 我知道你正在想什么，但是我不认为你应该告诉他这件事。

Better:

> 我知道你在想什么，但这事不该告诉他。

Do not retain English-like `我/你/他` in every clause.

### Abstract nouns and nominalization

Weak:

> 他的决定导致了她对这件事的理解产生了变化。

Better:

> 他这一决定，让她重新认识了这件事。

Prefer actions and concrete verbs.

### Stacked modifiers

Weak:

> 一个被所有人恐惧却从未真正理解的充满神秘力量的男人

Better:

> 一个众人畏惧、却从未真正理解的男人，身上有种神秘力量

If the line must be shortened, split the information or omit what the image already conveys.

### False causality

Do not turn English contrastive structures into overexplicit causality.

- `but` is not always `但是`; sometimes no connective is needed.
- `and` is not always `和`; Chinese often links by sequence.
- `so` may be implied by word order.
- `because` can sometimes be moved or omitted if the relation is clear.

### “的” chains

Weak:

> 这是关于一个失去了一切但仍然没有放弃希望的男人的故事。

Better:

> 一个人失去了一切，却始终没有放弃希望。

### Passive voice

Do not copy passive constructions mechanically.

Weak:

> 他被认为是一个没有能力保护自己家人的人。

Better:

> 人人都觉得，他连家人都保护不了。

### Explanatory completion

Do not make an implied object explicit if the ambiguity is intentional.

Weak:

> 他知道她已经背叛了他，所以才会那样说。

If the source only says "He knew," the clause after it may be invented.

## 3. Chinese sentence rhythm

Film lines benefit from:

- Short clauses.
- Concrete verbs.
- A clear final stress word.
- Pauses at dramatic beats.
- Repetition only when it carries emotion or rhetoric.

Avoid:

- Four-character idioms in every line.
- Written-language connectors such as `然而`, `因而`, `鉴于`, `就此事而言` unless the character is genuinely formal.
- Uniformly balanced sentence pairs that sound like slogans.
- Overly literary metaphors in casual dialogue.
- Modern internet abbreviations in period or prestige drama.

## 4. Pronouns and omitted subjects

Chinese can omit subjects and objects. Use that to create natural flow:

Source:

> I know what you did.

Options:

> 我知道你做了什么。

> 你做过什么，我知道。

> 我知道。

Choose based on dramatic emphasis and whether the object must be explicit.

When multiple male or female characters speak, avoid using `他` or `她` when it could cause speaker ambiguity. Repeat the name or a role term: `你父亲`, `王后`, `这家伙`, `那老头`.

## 5. “You,” address, and social relations

Chinese requires a decision that English often hides:

- 你 / 您.
- 名字 / 姓氏 / 全名.
- 职位 / 军衔 / 尊称.
- 亲属称谓.
- 侮辱性称呼.
- 亲昵称呼.

Do not default to `你` for every `you`.

Examples of relational meaning:

- A servant using `您` can signal distance or fear.
- A parent calling a child by full name can signal anger.
- A superior dropping a title and using a first name can signal intimacy or contempt.
- A character deliberately using a title after betrayal can signal emotional rupture.

Lock each relationship in the voice bible.

## 6. Names

Prefer:

1. Official localized name in the same release.
2. Recurring official franchise name.
3. Widely recognized standard Chinese name.
4. A consistent transliteration.

Avoid:

- Mixing `阿伽门农` and `阿加曼农`.
- Using transliterations from different regional conventions in one track.
- Literal translations of names unless the film uses them as meaning.
- Changing a nickname into a formal name without a relationship reason.

When a name is subjected to wordplay, preserve both pronunciation and meaning where possible.

## 7. Register and period language

Classify the film's verbal world:

- Contemporary casual.
- Contemporary formal/institutional.
- Historical epic.
- Fantasy/ritual.
- Literary or theatrical.
- Documentary/journalistic.
- Legal/medical/military.

Historical dialogue need not use archaic Chinese throughout. Use just enough register to establish the period while keeping comprehension immediate.

Avoid:

- Fake classical syntax that no actor could perform.
- Random archaic particles.
- Modern corporate language in a premodern setting.
- Formal written Chinese for a drunken argument.

## 8. Idioms and proverbs

Use a Chinese idiom only when:

- Its meaning is exact enough.
- Its register fits the scene.
- Its cultural image does not create a false setting.
- It does not make a simple line sound like a maxim.

Do not use `一箭双雕`, `说曹操曹操到`, or other culturally specific expressions in a world where those references would be impossible unless the localization deliberately uses domesticating language.

## 9. Profanity

Map intensity and social function.

Possible ranges:

- `该死`, `见鬼`, `妈的`, `混账`, `混蛋`.
- `王八蛋`, `畜生`, `杂种`.
- Sexual/religious/regional taboo terms only when rating and character justify them.

Do not:

- Translate every `fuck` as `操`.
- Make a frightened child speak like a hardened soldier.
- Censor a scene so heavily that the threat disappears.
- Use an insult that introduces an unrelated ethnic, sexual, or religious connotation absent from the source.

Check consistency across the character and film.

## 10. Questions, requests, and commands

Chinese can make a command harsher or softer with:

- `给我`
- `把……`
- `请你`
- `能不能`
- `要不要`
- `别`
- Sentence-final particles such as `吧`, `啊`, `呢`, `嘛`.

Do not preserve English question syntax when it sounds translated:

Weak:

> 你为什么要这样做？

Possible:

> 你这是干什么？

> 为什么？

> 你图什么？

Choose by character and situation.

## 11. Rhetorical questions and sarcasm

English rhetorical questions often sound too explicit in Chinese.

Source:

> Do you think I'm an idiot?

Options:

> 你觉得我傻吗？

> 当我傻？

> 你把我当什么了？

The shortest option may be stronger. Do not add `难道` unless the rhetorical force benefits.

## 12. Action, violence, and urgency

Use:

- Short verb-first lines.
- Clear direction and target.
- No redundant pronouns.
- Repeated commands for genuine urgency.

Examples:

> 退后！

> 左舷！

> 收桨！

> 别让他过去！

Do not replace technical orders with vague poetic language.

## 13. Songs and lyrics

Decide between:

- Literal meaning.
- Poetic image.
- Rhythmic line.
- Singable text.

For non-singable subtitles, keep:

- Repeated refrain.
- Key nouns and images.
- Line length.
- Emotional arc.

Avoid pseudo-classical language merely because the text is a song.

## 14. Punctuation

Common Chinese subtitle practice:

- Omit final full stops on ordinary declaratives.
- Retain question marks, exclamation marks, ellipses, and meaningful pauses.
- Use `……` for trailing speech rather than `...`.
- Use `——` for interruption or explanation only when it reflects the performance.
- Use `“”` for quoted speech.
- Use speaker dashes at line beginnings when two speakers share a cue.
- Do not add a period after `！` or `？`.

Follow the target platform if it has different rules.

## 15. Simplified versus Traditional Chinese

Lock:

- Character set.
- Regional vocabulary.
- Name transliteration system.
- Punctuation width.
- Quotation marks.

Do not mix:

- `伊萨卡` and `伊塔卡`.
- `阿伽门农` and `阿加曼农`.
- Simplified narration with traditional honorifics.
- Mainland and Taiwanese terms for the same institution unless intentionally marked.

If the user specifies Traditional Chinese, use the regional name and vocabulary convention consistently rather than converting character-by-character.

## 16. Bilingual subtitles

For bilingual deliverables:

- Decide which language is primary.
- Keep source and target on consistent lines.
- Do not let the source line dictate unnatural target line breaks.
- Ensure total height remains readable.
- Use one font or two deliberately.
- Do not duplicate information within each language.
- Preserve timing even if the combined cue is dense; reduce wording before reducing font.

## 17. Chinese-specific final checks

Search for:

- Repeated `的`.
- Repeated `我`.
- Overuse of `他/她`.
- `一个` from English articles.
- `是……的` constructions.
- Overuse of `被`.
- Stiff `当……的时候`.
- `对于……来说` when a simpler topic structure works.
- Literal `你知道` / `你知道的`.
- Multiple names for one person.
- Mixed simplified/traditional characters.
- Inconsistent use of `您` and `你`.
- Full stops at the end of ordinary subtitle lines.

Read the line aloud mentally. If an actor would pause unnaturally or the line sounds like an information caption rather than dialogue, rewrite it.
